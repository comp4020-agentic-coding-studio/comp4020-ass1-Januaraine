Your arm sets the target

The camera doesn't see a hand. It sees landmarks. The tracker estimates shoulder, elbow, wrist, and fingertip points on your arm from the camera image, then maps the fingertip into a 3D target — this is a mapping of tracked landmarks into the robot's workspace, not metric 3D tracking. The IK solver then finds the joint angles that reach that target.

How it works

FK is a direct calculation: reach = L1·cos θ1 + L2·cos(θ1+θ2) + L3·cos(θ1+θ2+θ3), and the same pattern with sines for height. Plug in angles, get exactly one position back.

IK runs the other way: given a target position, it repeatedly nudges the angles by Δθ = gain · Jᵀ · error — the transpose of that same Jacobian times how far off the current guess is — until the error shrinks close to zero. It's a search, not a formula, and it can take several steps, or never fully converge if the target sits outside the arm's reach.

Where this shows up

Da Vinci surgical robots: the surgeon moves a handle to a position and orientation; the console solves inverse kinematics dozens of times a second to find the instrument-arm joint angles that reach it — the same θ1/θ2/θ3 solve above, with more joints.

Factory welding arms: a planned weld seam is a path of target poses in space, and the controller runs an IK solve at every waypoint to turn that path into a joint-angle trajectory the motors can actually execute.

Try this

Switch to Inverse Kinematics and move your hand far out to the side or behind you — watch the dot in the Workspace panel leave the blue disc and turn red as the target passes the arm's 2.4 m reach.

Switch to Forward Kinematics and drag θ2 (elbow) to its limit — the elbow stops exactly there, because nothing is being solved for; the angle you set is the angle it gets.

Key takeaway

Forward kinematics: angles are known, position is computed — one formula, one answer, every time.

Inverse kinematics: a position is wanted, angles are solved for — iteratively, every frame, and not always with a single unique answer.
